Feelings to Numbers

Every step so far, you judged quality by reading a handful of printed results and thinking "yeah, that looks about right." That works when you're staring at 3 examples. It quietly falls apart at any real scale — a pipeline can look perfect on the 3 things you happened to check and still fail on 30% of everything else, and you'd never know, because you never looked at the other 30%.

step8_evaluation.py replaces that gut-check with numbers. It writes down 6 questions and their correct answers in advance (the "golden dataset"), then runs every chunking strategy from step 7 against that fixed test and scores each one — automatically, the same way, every time. Every number on this page is real output from one actual run of that script.

TWO COMPLETELY SEPARATE THINGS GET MEASURED HERE

1. Retrieval quality — did the search step find the right chunk of text at all? This is fast to check and doesn't need an LLM: you already know what the right chunk should contain, so it's just "is that text in there, yes or no, and how good was its position?"

2. Answer quality — given whatever chunks were found, did the model's final written answer actually make sense — is it accurate, and does it address the question? This needs a second LLM call to judge, because "is this a good sentence" isn't a substring check.

A pipeline can ace #1 and still botch #2 (the right facts, badly written), or the reverse (a fluent, confident answer built on the wrong chunk). Mixing the two up is the single most common evaluation mistake — this step keeps them separate on purpose.

What does "finding the right answer" actually mean?

For each of the 6 golden questions, the script also wrote down an expected_phrase — a short, distinctive snippet of text that must appear in whichever chunk correctly answers that question. That turns "did retrieval work?" into a fast, deterministic check: is this exact substring present in the text that came back, yes or no. No eyeballing, no LLM call needed for this part.

But "is it present" isn't the whole story — where it shows up in the results, and how much irrelevant text it's buried among, both matter too. Here's one real question run against two different chunking strategies from step 7, showing all three retrieval metrics on the exact same successful search.

Hit@k "Is the right answer anywhere in what I retrieved?" 1 if found in top k, else 0 Binary — a yes/no smoke test. Both strategies above score 1: the phrase showed up somewhere in their top 3. It doesn't care whether that was rank 1 or rank 3 — which is exactly why it can't separate a great retrieval from a barely-passing one.
MRR "How far down the list did I have to look?" average of 1 / rank Rewards finding it early. Rank 1 scores a full 1.00; rank 2 scores 0.50; rank 3 scores 0.33 — a steep, deliberate penalty. Fixed's hit at rank 3 (0.33) and paragraph's hit at rank 1 (1.00) is where these two strategies actually stop looking the same.
Precision@k "Of what I handed back, how much was actually useful?" relevant chunks retrieved / k Measures noise. Fixed handed back 1 useful chunk out of 3 (0.33) — two wasted slots the model still has to read past. Paragraph handed back 2 of 3 (0.67), because its near-duplicate chunk at rank 2 also happened to contain the phrase.

The full procedure — what the script actually does, in order

With the three metrics defined, here's the actual sequence step8_evaluation.py runs through, top to bottom. The key design choice: retrieval metrics are cheap (a substring check — no LLM needed), but answer-quality judging is expensive (a second LLM call per question, on top of generating the answer itself). So the script spends the expensive step only once, on whichever strategy already won the cheap comparison.

The outcome — and how much to trust it

Paragraph-aware chunking won the retrieval comparison clearly, so it's the strategy that went on to the expensive answer-quality step: Faithfulness = 0.42, Relevance = 0.85 (Fig. 2). Relevance is straightforwardly good — the model's answers address the question. Faithfulness looks concerning — under half — but before treating that number as ground truth, it's worth checking whether the judge itself is reliable.

The script actually re-runs generation and judging a second time, independently, in its per-question breakdown (Part C) — same 6 questions, same winning strategy, same judge model that already produced the 0.42 above. Here's what that second, separate pass found.

So what should you actually take away from this step? Not "Faithfulness is 0.42, this pipeline is bad" — and not "Faithfulness is 0.77, this pipeline is fine" either. The honest reading is: retrieval quality (MRR, Hit@k, Precision@k) is measured with plain substring checks, so those numbers are solid and reproducible — trust them. Answer quality, scored by a second LLM call, is a genuinely useful signal but a noisy one — treat a single Faithfulness or Relevance number as a rough estimate, not a verdict, and re-run it before making a real decision based on it.

Worth noticing, not yet proving Question 1's answer reads as completely grounded — it restates exactly what the retrieved chunk says — yet one judging pass scored it Faithfulness = 0.00 while the other pass's average landed at 0.42. Both numbers came from the same model (llama3.2:3b) generating the answer and judging it — a model's blind spots in generation can correlate with its blind spots in judging its own output. And separately, expected_phrase is hand-tied to this exact document's wording — edit DOCUMENT in step 7 without updating GOLDEN here, and every retrieval metric would silently read as failure for no real reason.
